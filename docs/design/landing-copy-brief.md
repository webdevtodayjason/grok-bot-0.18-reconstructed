# Copywriting brief: titanium.bot landing page

You are writing the marketing copy for Titanium Bot, sold by Titanium Computing (an Austin-area MSP) to business
owners and to the MSP's own clients. The current draft was written from the software's point of view and reads
like computer talk. The owner's verdict on its headline "Workers with their own computers": "That's not the
headline. That's not good marketing. What is this to a business owner?" Write for the business owner.

## What the product is, in the owner's words
"They're getting virtual AI assistants, virtual workforces, and AI agents that can do work for you: pick up your
repetitive tasks, answer emails, create workflows, do marketing. If you can do it on a computer, you can teach our
agent exactly how to do the job, let it make its own routine around your screen recording, and let the agent take
over that job."

## What is true today (do not invent features; everything below exists and was measured on the owner's own instance)
- Each customer gets their own private instance, run on Titanium's own servers, with AI agents they name (the first is
  usually a chief of staff). Every agent has its own computer: a real Linux desktop with Chrome, files and a shell.
- Agents work on a schedule around the clock (routines such as a 7:15 weekday morning brief, a ticket dispatch every
  30 minutes, a long-ticket watch every 2 hours), and on request in a chat.
- You can teach an agent a task by doing it once while it watches (screen recording); it saves the recipe as a skill and
  runs it on its own afterwards.
- Agents sign in to your tools and use them the way a person would (a browser, email, Slack, GitHub, Linear, Google, a
  ticketing system), through a Marketplace of connectors.
- When an agent needs a password or an API key, it asks with a secure card; you paste it once and the value goes into
  the agent's own locked store, never through the AI model and never into the chat.
- Every reply carries a receipt count ("Backed by 7 tool results") computed by the system from what the agent
  actually ran, so an agent cannot report work it did not do.
- Your data stays on your instance (its own containers, volumes, password and address); nightly backups with a
  restore drill; you bring your own AI model keys (OpenAI-compatible, Grok, or a local model), so no usage caps set
  by a chatbot vendor. A managed-inference plan is planned, not live.
- One instance can hand attested jobs to another (a job bus), so a client's agents can work with the MSP's agents.
- Sold and supported by an MSP: setup, credentials and monitoring are handled with you, not left to you.

## What the competition says (study these, then beat them on clarity, not on adjectives)
- x.ai/bot (Grok Bot): "AI teammates that finish the work." "Bots can sign in to your tools, use them just like you
  do, and come back with finished work." "Show a Bot how it's done." "Work with many Bots at once." Full text in
  references.txt.
- vellum.ai: "Your Personal Intelligence. A personal AI assistant that remembers how you work, learns your preferences,
  and takes action across the tools you already use."
- argentos.ai: "A self-hosted AI operating system with persistent memory, autonomous thinking, voice interaction,
  and multi-channel communication."
- Buyer personas from a 2026 category write-up: the operator who delegates recurring work; the privacy-minded user
  who wants self-hosted control; the model switcher; the communication-heavy professional who wants one assistant
  reachable everywhere; the technical builder. Why people look past Grok Bot: access cost tied to premium plans,
  cloud-only storage, one model provider, coarse permissions, few channels.

## What to write (deliver as Markdown, one file, no em dashes, no exclamation marks, no "unleash" / "supercharge" /
## "seamless" / "revolutionize", plain sentences a business owner would say out loud)
1. Five headline options (each with a one-line subhead), ranked, each answering "what is this to a business owner".
2. The hero paragraph (two or three sentences) and the two button labels.
3. "How it works" in three steps, each step two sentences, from the owner's chair.
4. "What it takes off your plate": six short items with a concrete example each (tickets, email, reports, follow-ups,
   research, marketing), each one thing an owner recognises.
5. "Why you can trust it": five items, plain language, covering receipts, credentials, your data on your own instance,
   backups, and that a person at Titanium is on the other end.
6. Pricing section copy for three cards: bring-your-own-keys per instance per month; managed inference (coming);
   MSP partner plan (talk to us). Numbers are not set; write the shape.
7. FAQ: eight questions a skeptical owner asks (does it replace staff, what if it makes a mistake, where is my data,
   what does it cost to run, what can it not do, how long to set up, can I see what it did, can I stop it), with
   honest answers from the facts above.
8. A closing call to action (two lines) and a one-line footer tagline that echoes but does not copy Titanium
   Computing's "The element needed to keep your business running".
Keep every claim inside the facts above. Where something is planned rather than live, say "coming", never "now".
